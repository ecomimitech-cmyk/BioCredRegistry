2. The demo — how to open it

Nothing to install and nothing to configure. You open a web address, and you use two test logins I will send separately from this document.

Start here

https://biocred-staging-demo.netlify.app

Two logins, sent separately: one biologist account and one reviewer account. They are staging test users and can be deleted at any time. Credentials never travel in the same message as the link.

Each step below is a direct link. About twenty minutes end to end, and you can stop after step 2 and still have seen the thing that matters.

  1. Open the approved public profile · 3 min
    Registry ID SM7523. The name reads “M2 Smoke Biologist” and the entries are called things like “Survey 1” — that is a test account, and the wording is deliberately made up. What is not made up is the path: every field on that page was entered by a user, read by a reviewer, approved, and published by the system. None of it is typed into the page.
  2. Open PEND99 · 1 min
    It must refuse. A profile that has not been approved does not exist publicly, and seeing that refusal is more informative than seeing the profile that works.
  3. Sign in as the reviewer · 8 min · use the reviewer account
    The queue loads. Open a submission, open one of its documents, and look at the record of decisions underneath.
  4. Sign in again as the biologist, then open that same reviewer page · 2 min · use the biologist account
    You get a locked gate and no data. That is the access model doing its job, and it is worth thirty seconds of your time.
  5. Optional — submit a fresh draft as the biologist · 10 min · use the biologist account
    Then approve it as the reviewer and watch it appear publicly. That full loop is the product.
Please do not sign up anyone real yet — your supervisor included. This is staging, populated with test records, and I would rather your first real user arrived on a production environment with your domain on it.
If a page looks broken. The staging database is on the free plan and goes to sleep after a quiet stretch. The first visit after that can fail or hang. It is not a fault in the system and nothing is lost — tell me and I will wake it, which takes under a minute. I checked every link in this document on the day I sent it.

3. What you will see, screen by screen

Screen 1 · Public profile — the thing a firm or an agency would look at

Opened with a registry ID in the address, e.g. ?id=SM7523. Live data, read straight from the registry.

  • Name, title, location, and the “Documentation Verified” badge
  • The experience map across the five domains, with the standing caveat that it is not a score
  • Profile insights in plain sentences — for this profile, experience concentrated in Field & Applied Practice, sparse in Knowledge & Research
  • Credential history: 3 verified credentials, each with issuer and year
  • Experience evidence: 8 verified records, grouped by domain
  • No documents, no personal contact details, no date of birth — none of that is exposed publicly by design
Screen 2 · Reviewer portal — the queue and the decision

Reached by signing in with the reviewer account. A biologist signing into the same address sees a locked gate instead.

  • The queue of people waiting to be reviewed
  • The submission itself: profile fields, credentials, experience records
  • Open on a document produces a link that works for five minutes and then stops working. The storage stays private throughout; the reviewer never receives a permanent address for a biologist's file
  • Start review moves the submission to under verification
  • Verify / Partial / Reject. A rejection with an empty reason box is refused — not by the page being polite, but by the database declining to perform it
  • The audit trail beneath: one row per record changed, each carrying who decided, what changed, when, and the reason they gave
Screen 3 · Biologist intake — how a submission gets there in the first place

This is Milestone 2, included so the chain is walkable in one sitting.

  • Create an account or sign in
  • Fill the draft profile and save it
  • Add credentials, and upload a document for one of them
  • Add experience records
  • Submit for review — the status becomes pending_review and the submission appears in the reviewer's queue

5. Who can see what

This is the part I would most like you to test adversarially, because a credentials registry is worth exactly as much as its access rules. Every rule below is enforced in the database, not in the page. That distinction matters: a hidden button is a courtesy, whereas a database rule holds even against someone bypassing the interface entirely.

Who Can see Cannot see or do
Anyone, not signed in Approved public profiles only, and only the public-safe fields: name, title, location, credentials, experience summary, the map. Any pending, rejected or draft profile. Any uploaded document. The audit log. Anything about who reviewed what.
Biologist Their own profile, their own documents, their own submission status. Anyone else's submission or documents. The reviewer queue. Any decision at all — attempting one comes back refused, for the stated reason that they are not a reviewer. And they cannot make themselves one.
Reviewer The queue of submitted profiles, their credentials and experience, and their documents through five-minute links. The audit trail. Rewriting or deleting an audit entry — the log accepts inserts and reads, and nothing else. Deciding twice on the same submission. Rejecting without stating a reason.
Owner (you and I) The Supabase dashboard: tables, storage, users, logs. Nothing is hidden from an owner — which is precisely why reviewer status is granted deliberately, from the dashboard, and there is no path from any web page to becoming one.
Worth testing yourself: sign in as the biologist and open the reviewer address directly. You should get the gate and nothing else. That single check tells you more about the system than the approved profile does.

6. Milestone 3 — what was built and how

The security half of this milestone already existed from earlier work: the roles table, the rule that decides whether someone is a reviewer, the read and write permissions on submitted work, and an audit log deliberately built so that entries can be added and read but never edited or removed. What was missing was the screen. The reviewer page was three hundred lines of prototype with invented rows in it and no connection to the database at all.

What I added

PieceWhat it does
Picking up a fileMoves a submission from waiting for review to being checked, so two reviewers cannot silently work the same file at once.
Making the decisionApprove, partly approve, or reject. It refuses someone who is not a reviewer. It refuses a rejection with no reason written. It refuses a file that was already decided. And it makes a profile public only on approval — no other route sets that.
Granting reviewer statusMakes someone a reviewer. Deliberately available to nobody through the website, so it runs only from the dashboard. There is no path from any page to promoting yourself.
The reviewer page itselfRebuilt against real data: the real queue, the real submission, five-minute document links, the decision record, and the locked gate for everyone else.

A bug found on the way, worth your attention

The audit log was recording that a status had changed but not why — every entry carried the placeholder text auto_trigger. For a registry whose value to an agency is the paper trail, an audit log that cannot say why a decision was made is most of the point missing.

Decisions now carry the reviewer's actual stated reason onto the audit row. And a status changed by any route other than the proper review path still records as unexplained — which is the right outcome, because it means an auditor can tell the difference between a decision somebody justified and a change nobody did.

7. Milestone 4 — what was built and how

Two halves of this system had never been introduced to each other. The engine that computes a profile's experience map from its evidence existed and was tested, but had never written to the database. The public page existed but was a mockup with a verification date typed into the markup by hand. Milestone 4 is the join.

Approved submissionReviewer verifies it
→
Publishing stepRebuilds the public version
→
Public versionStored, ready to show
→
Public profileShareable address + map
PieceWhat it does
The publishing stepTakes every profile allowed to be published, runs it through the engine that turns evidence into a map, and stores the result. Run against staging, it published six profiles including SM7523. Running it a second time changes nothing — which is the behaviour you want from something that may run on a schedule.
The public page, wired to real dataIt now reads the actual profile, credentials, experience and map from the registry. The verification date that used to be typed in by hand is gone.
Knowing when to republish, without new machineryA stored public version counts as out of date when it is missing, or older than the profile it came from — and a review decision already touches the profile. So approving something marks it for republishing as a side effect. No waiting list to maintain, no extra moving parts to go wrong.
A second lock on the same doorA visitor who is not signed in can now read the public views and do nothing else. Trying to give themselves a role, or to read the decision record, is refused outright. The protective rules were already there; this simply makes the refusal explicit rather than incidental.
On the engine staying where it is. The engine that turns evidence into an experience map stays on the server rather than being rewritten to run inside the visitor's browser. That keeps one version of the rules and the tests already written against them, and it honours the promise made in July. It also means the rules that decide what a map looks like cannot be read, copied or altered by whoever opens the page — they never leave the server.

9. What this package does not claim

The same discipline as the Milestone 2 package, because the value of these documents depends on their being trustworthy about their own limits.

DemonstratedNot demonstrated
Real accounts, going through the real browser pages, against the real staging database and real private storage — with the rules enforced in the database rather than in the page. Load, many users at once, hostile input, or production hardening. This is staging on a free plan with test data, and it should keep being described that way to anyone outside the two of us.

Nothing in this package makes anyone employable, certified or endorsed. A public profile says that documentation was submitted and checked. It does not say the person is good at their job, and the wording on every screen is chosen to keep that line clear.

10. Known limitations

Stated plainly, because you will find them anyway and I would rather you heard them from me.

LimitationWhat it means for you
A rejected submission cannot be resubmittedThe biologist cannot currently reopen and correct a rejected profile. It is a real workflow gap and belongs in the next milestone.
Decisions apply to the whole submissionA reviewer cannot approve two credentials and reject a third. It is one verdict per submission for now.
File checking is by extension onlyThe system trusts that a file named as a PDF is one. Real content inspection is production hardening, not staging work.
Publishing is a job, not an instantApproval marks a profile as needing recomputation; the refresh job performs it. On staging I run it by hand. In production it would run on a schedule, so a public profile could lag its approval by minutes.
Staging sleepsFree plan. After a quiet period the database pauses and the first visit fails until it wakes. If a link ever looks broken, that is almost always why — tell me and I will wake it.
New signups may need confirmingStaging may ask a new account to confirm its email. For test accounts I confirm them from the dashboard.
No production environment yetYour domain is untouched. Moving to production is its own piece of work: a paid database, a real address, and hardening before any real person signs up.
